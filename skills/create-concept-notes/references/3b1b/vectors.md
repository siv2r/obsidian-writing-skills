# Vectors (3Blue1Brown)

## Opening excerpt

> The fundamental building block for linear algebra is the vector, so it's worth making sure we're all on the same page about what exactly a vector is. Broadly speaking there are three distinct-but-related interpretations of vectors, which I'll call the physics student perspective, the computer science perspective, and the mathematician's perspective. The physics student perspective is that vectors are arrows pointing in space. What defines a given vector is its length and the direction it's pointing, but as long as those two facts are the same you can move it around and it's still the same vector.

## Distinctive excerpt

> Each vector represents a certain movement; a step with a certain distance and direction. If you take a step along the first vector, then take a step in the direction and distance described by the second vector, the overall effect is the same as if it just moved along the sum of those two vectors.

## Why this reference is here

This lesson is the cleanest example of Pattern 6 — the disarming "make sure we're all on the same page" meta-opener. Rather than posturing as if the reader already knows (or doesn't), Grant acknowledges explicitly that "vector" means different things to different people, and invites the reader into a shared starting frame before any definition lands. That single phrase does enormous load-bearing work for register: it signals that the lesson is a conversation, not a lecture.

It also demonstrates Pattern 2 — action first, name second. Vector addition is introduced by the *behavior* ("take a step along the first vector, then take a step in the direction and distance described by the second") before any symbolic notation or the word "sum" carries weight. The motion is the definition; the name is the label attached afterwards.

## Voice observations

- Register is conversational and slightly hedged ("broadly speaking", "pretty much a fancy word for list") — never chummy, never stiff.
- Math-to-prose integration: multiple viewpoints are narrated in parallel before any one is privileged, which models how to handle a concept that has several legitimate definitions.
- Reader address uses "you" frequently but always in a procedural sense ("if you were doing some analytics...", "you'd be modeling...") — it puts the reader inside the example rather than flattering them.

## Global 3Blue1Brown caveats

- Prefer opening paragraphs and verbal definitions over mid-lesson "as you can see" moments that reference animations.
- Calibrate second-person address DOWN a notch — 3B1B uses more "you" than crypto concept notes should.
