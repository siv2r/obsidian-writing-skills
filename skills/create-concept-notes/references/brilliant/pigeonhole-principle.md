# Pigeonhole Principle (Brilliant wiki)

## Opening excerpt

> Consider a flock of pigeons nestled in a set of $n$ pigeonholes. If there are $n$ pigeons, then it is possible for all of the pigeons to rest happily in separate pigeonholes. However, if at least one more pigeon arrives, making a total of *more than* $n$ pigeons, then at least one of the pigeonholes, inevitably, will end up with more than one pigeon.
>
> The idea that having more pigeons than pigeonholes requires a pigeonhole with more than one pigeon is seemingly trivial, but it turns out to be important enough that it has a name:
>
> > **Pigeonhole Principle (naive form):**
> > If more than $n$ objects are placed into $n$ boxes, then at least one box must contain more than one object.

## Distinctive excerpt

> At first glance, the **pigeonhole principle** (also known as **Dirichlet's principle** in honor of the eponymous German mathematician) might appear to be too obvious to be useful; indeed, the power of the principle comes from cleverly choosing the "boxes" and "objects." Even though the principle itself is quite simple, it is not always clear if it is useful and, if so, how. For instance, consider the following example:
>
> > A box contains three pairs of socks colored red, blue, and white, respectively. Suppose I take out the socks without looking at them. How many socks must I take out to be sure that they will include a matching pair?
>
> [Later, generalized form:] If more than $k \cdot n$ objects are placed into $n$ boxes then at least one box must contain more than $k$ objects.

## Why this reference is here

The article is the reference-set exemplar of the **narrative scene → formalism → hands-on puzzle** shape. It opens with pigeons and pigeonholes as a scene the reader can literally picture, not as an abstract combinatorial setup. Only after the scene is in the reader's head does the article promote the observation to a named principle inside a callout. The formalism arrives as the *third* move, not the first.

It also demonstrates **Pattern 3** in its hook form: the article names the reader's likely skepticism directly — "might appear to be too obvious to be useful" — and uses that skepticism as the wedge into the interesting claim, which is that the principle's power lies in the cleverness of the mapping, not in the principle itself. The sock-puzzle immediately after converts the abstract worry ("how could this be useful?") into something the reader can actually work. The generalized k-version shows up only once the naive version has been used, preserving the pedagogical order: minimal form first, strengthen later.

## Voice observations

- Narrative verbs ("nestled", "rest happily", "inevitably") give the setup a storybook register that lowers the reader's guard before the formal statement.
- Math-prose integration uses italics for emphasis (*more than*) inside prose rather than display equations. The only displayed object in the opening is the named principle itself, quoted in a callout — the formalism is presented as the payoff of the scene, not as a prerequisite to it.
- Reader address is first-person puzzle-voice ("Suppose I take out the socks without looking at them"), which invites the reader into the scenario as participant rather than observer.
