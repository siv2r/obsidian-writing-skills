# Bayes' Theorem (Brilliant wiki)

## Opening excerpt

> **Bayes' theorem** is a formula that describes how to update the probabilities of hypotheses when given evidence. It follows simply from the axioms of conditional probability, but can be used to powerfully reason about a wide range of problems involving belief updates.

## Distinctive excerpt

> Many medical diagnostic tests are said to be X% accurate, for instance 99% accurate, referring specifically to the probability that the test result is correct given your condition (or lack thereof). This is not the same as the posterior probability of having the disease given the result of the test.
>
> Probability problems are notorious for yielding surprising and counterintuitive results.
>
> Your first instinct to this question might be to answer 1/3, since this is obviously the same question as the previous one. Knowing the day of the week a child is born on can't possibly give you additional information, right?

## Why this reference is here

The opening is a textbook **Pattern 1 (functional definition)**: Bayes' theorem is introduced not as an equation but as a thing-that-does-something — "a formula that describes how to update the probabilities of hypotheses when given evidence." The definition tells the reader what the theorem is *for* before showing them how it works, and the second sentence places it relative to its foundation (axioms of conditional probability) and its payoff (reasoning about belief updates). The formula itself is deferred. The reader arrives at the math already knowing what it is supposed to accomplish.

The article is also a strong example of **Pattern 3 (names likely reader confusion)**. The medical-testing passage anticipates exactly the misreading most readers carry in — that "99% accurate" means "99% chance I have the disease given a positive result" — and marks the gap explicitly. The Tuesday-boy paragraph does the same move at the meta level: it voices the reader's likely objection ("can't possibly give you additional information, right?") and uses the reader's own resistance as the hook into the surprising answer.

## Voice observations

- Short declarative openings. The first sentence is twelve words and carries the whole definition.
- Math-prose integration is deferred: the opening paragraph uses zero symbols. The reader is anchored in ordinary English before any P(A|B) appears.
- Reader address is direct and conversational in the distinctive passages ("Your first instinct to this question might be..."), which works because it narrates the reader's thought process rather than lecturing them.
